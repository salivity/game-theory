What is an Evolutionarily Stable Strategy?

An Evolutionarily Stable Strategy (ESS) is a fundamental concept in biological game theory that explains how specific behaviors and traits persist in a population over time. This article provides a clear, direct overview of what an ESS is, how it differs from classical game theory, the mathematical conditions required to achieve it, and real-world examples of how it functions in nature.

Understanding the Concept of ESS

Coined by biologist John Maynard Smith and scientist George R. Price in 1973, an Evolutionarily Stable Strategy is defined as a strategy which, if adopted by most members of a population, cannot be invaded by any alternative "mutant" strategy.

In traditional game theory, players make conscious, rational choices to maximize their payoffs. In biological game theory, however, the "players" are organisms, the "strategies" are genetically inherited traits or behaviors, and the "payoff" is evolutionary fitness (survival and reproductive success). Over generations, natural selection filters out strategies with lower payoffs, leaving the most successful behaviors dominant.

The Mathematical Conditions of Stability

For a strategy (\(I\)) to be evolutionarily stable against a rare mutant strategy (\(J\)), it must satisfy specific conditions regarding its expected payoff (represented as \(E\)). There are two pathways to stability:

  1. Strict Superiority: The dominant strategy must perform better against itself than the mutant strategy does against the dominant strategy. \[E(I, I) > E(J, I)\]

  2. Alternative Superiority: If the mutant strategy performs equally well against the dominant strategy, the dominant strategy must perform better against the mutant than the mutant does against itself. \[\text{If } E(I, I) = E(J, I), \text{ then } E(I, J) > E(J, J)\]

If either of these conditions is met, natural selection will prevent the mutant strategy from spreading through the population, keeping the dominant strategy stable.

A Classic Example: The Hawk-Dove Game

The dynamics of an ESS are best illustrated by the classic "Hawk-Dove" game, which models animal conflict over a shared resource:

  • Hawks (Aggressive): Always fight for the resource, risking injury.
  • Doves (Peaceful): Display and share the resource, retreating if a fight starts.

If a population consists entirely of Doves, a mutant Hawk will easily invade because it wins every conflict without resistance. Conversely, if a population consists entirely of Hawks, the cost of constant injury becomes so high that a mutant Dove (which avoids injury) can successfully invade.

Consequently, neither pure Hawk nor pure Dove is an ESS. Instead, the Evolutionarily Stable Strategy is a mixture of both behaviors. This can manifest as a "mixed ESS," where individuals randomize their behavior, or a stable polymorphism, where a fixed percentage of the population acts as Hawks and the remaining percentage acts as Doves.

Why ESS Matters in Biology

The concept of an ESS explains many natural phenomena that seem counterintuitive at first glance. It helps biologists understand why animals do not always fight to the death over mates or territory, why altruistic behaviors exist, and why diverse mating strategies can coexist within a single species. By analyzing these behaviors through the lens of evolutionary stability, scientists can predict how populations will adapt to changing environments and human interventions.
